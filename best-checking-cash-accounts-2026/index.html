The number nobody calculates

Your bank charges you in two ways, and only one of them shows up as a "fee." The visible costs are the monthly maintenance charge, overdraft and non-sufficient-funds (NSF) fees, and out-of-network ATM fees. Overdraft is the best documented: in market monitoring between December 2022 and August 2023, the CFPB found a median overdraft fee of $35 among the 119 banks and credit unions with more than $10 billion in assets that charged one, and a median NSF fee of $32. Monthly and ATM fees vary by bank and account; the table above shows what two large banks charge today.

Overdraft costs are also concentrated. The CFPB reported that nearly 80% of combined overdraft and NSF fees were paid by fewer than 9% of accountholders, those with more than 10 overdrafts a year. At a $35 fee, 11 overdrafts a year would cost $385.

The invisible cost is the interest you give up. The FDIC's national average rate was 0.07% on interest checking and 0.39% on savings (the $2,500 tier) as of November 16, 2026. If you keep $8,000 in an account paying 0.07% while a savings account pays 3.2%, you give up about $250 a year in interest (before compounding). That is not a fee on any statement; it is money that never shows up. Add the two together and you get the real cost of staying put.

The cost is largely avoidable. Ally charges $0 monthly maintenance and $0 overdraft item fees on its Spending account, and Capital One 360 Checking has no monthly cycle service charge. The hard part is not finding a better account; it is calculating what your current one costs so the switch feels worth the twenty minutes.

What actually makes an account worth switching to

Once you know your number, the choice gets simpler. A good everyday account clears three bars.

First, no monthly fee, no strings. Not "no fee if you keep $1,500 in it" or "no fee with direct deposit", just no fee. Ally's Spending account and Capital One 360 Checking both list a $0 monthly fee with no minimum balance to keep it.

Second, cash that earns something. Checking accounts pay little (Ally's Spending account pays 0.10% under $15,000 and 0.25% above, as of November 24, 2026), so the best setup is usually a no-fee checking account for spending plus a high-yield savings account for everything you do not need this month. Ally's savings account paid 3.2% variable on that date, against the FDIC's 0.39% national savings average.

Third, cheap overdraft and cash access. Ally charges $0 overdraft item fees and reimburses up to $10 per statement cycle of other banks' ATM fees; Capital One lists 70,000+ fee-free ATMs and a no-fee overdraft option if you regularly deposit at least $250. Read the conditions: they decide whether the fee really is $0 for you.

Frequently asked questions

How much does the average bank account cost per year?

There is no single official figure. Monthly fees, overdrafts and ATM fees vary by bank, and the CFPB's overdraft work shows the cost is concentrated: nearly 80% of overdraft and NSF fees fall on fewer than 9% of accountholders. Use the calculator with your own numbers rather than an average.

How much interest do I lose by keeping cash in checking?

As of November 16, 2026, the FDIC national average was 0.07% on interest checking and 0.39% on savings. Against a 3.2% savings account, $8,000 earns about $250 more per year, before compounding. The gap scales with your balance, and the savings rate is variable.

What is a typical overdraft fee?

The CFPB found a median overdraft fee of $35 at the 119 banks and credit unions with more than $10 billion in assets that charged one (December 2022 to August 2023). Some banks charge $0: Ally lists $0 overdraft item fees. Check your own bank's fee schedule.

Are fee-free ATM networks really free?

Only inside the network and under the account's conditions. Capital One lists 70,000+ fee-free ATMs across three networks, and Ally reimburses up to $10 per statement cycle of other banks' ATM fees. Outside those limits, the ATM owner and your bank can each charge.
