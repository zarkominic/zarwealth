What Is a Money Market Account?

A money market account (MMA) is a deposit account at a bank or credit union that typically pays more than a basic savings account. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says these accounts are insured by the FDIC or NCUA up to $250,000 held by the same owner or owners. That insurance is the difference that matters, because it is what protects your principal if the institution fails.

Key characteristics:

  • Usually pays more than a regular savings account, with a variable rate the bank can change
  • Insured by the FDIC (banks) or NCUA (credit unions), up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured institution, per ownership category
  • Often limits how many transactions you can make by check, debit card or electronic transfer; the CFPB notes ATM and in-person withdrawals are generally unlimited
  • Often comes with a debit card or check-writing access

Money market account vs money market fund

Money market accounts are different from money market funds. A money market fund is an investment sold by brokerage firms and fund companies, regulated by the SEC, that buys short-term securities. The CFPB describes it as an investment, not a savings or checking account. The FDIC and NCUA do not insure it, and SIPC, which covers a brokerage that fails, "does not protect against the decline in value of your securities." Funds aim to hold a steady $1.00 share price, but fund prospectuses say that is not guaranteed and an investment in the fund can lose money. So "zero risk" describes an insured account up to the coverage limit, not money market funds, which is why we changed this article's title.

  • Where to check a money market account: the bank's own page shows the APY, the minimum and any fees, and the FDIC's BankFind or the NCUA's credit union locator confirms the institution is insured.
  • Where to check a money market fund: the fund's own page or prospectus shows its 7-day yield (the income earned over the last seven days, annualized) and its expense ratio (the yearly fee as a percentage of assets). A 7-day yield is not a guaranteed rate.

1. Vanguard Cash Plus Account (check the live rate)

Vanguard's Cash Plus Account is a brokerage cash account, not a bank money market account. Its bank sweep holds your cash at one or more program banks, where it is FDIC insured up to $250,000 per insurable category of ownership at each program bank, subject to Vanguard's stated limits and conditions. Vanguard also offers its own money market funds in the account, and those are not FDIC insured. Vanguard's page lists a $0 minimum for the bank sweep and $3,000 for each money market fund. We did not quote a rate: the page showed no live APY when we checked, and we removed the old 4.70% figure and the old $1.25 million coverage claim because we could not confirm either.

Best for: Vanguard investors who want to keep cash next to their investments. Know which of the two cash options you are holding, because the insurance is different.

2. Sallie Mae Money Market Account: 3.60% APY

Sallie Mae's page shows a 3.60% APY on November 28, 2026 (variable, and it can change after you open the account). It states there is no minimum deposit to open and no monthly maintenance fees, and that deposits are FDIC insured up to $250,000 per depositor.

Best for: savers who want a bank money market account with no minimum and no monthly fee.

3. Ally Bank Money Market Account: 3.20% APY

Ally's page shows 3.20% APY, accurate as of November 24, 2026 (variable). There is no minimum balance to earn the APY, a $0 monthly maintenance fee, and the account comes with a debit card and checks. The catch is the transaction limit: Ally counts online and mobile transfers, checks and debit card purchases together, 10 per statement cycle. It says it does not charge a fee for going over, but it will close the account if you exceed the limit on more than an occasional basis. Deposits are unlimited, and Ally reimburses up to $10 per statement cycle in fees at other ATMs.

Best for: people who want a debit card and checks on a money market account and will stay under the transaction cap.

Also in the table: Wealthfront Cash Account

Wealthfront is not a bank money market account. It spreads deposits across program banks, which is how it reaches its stated $8 million of FDIC insurance eligibility (each bank provides its own $250,000). The 3.55% APY in our table is its base rate as of November 13, 2026; Wealthfront can change it, and promotional boosts, which we do not quote, may apply on top.

When to Use a Money Market Account

Money market accounts are a good fit for:

Emergency fund: you want the money safe and reachable. An account with a debit card or checks means you can get to it without waiting for a transfer, as long as you watch the transaction limit.

Short-term savings goals: saving for a trip, a car or a down payment over the next 6 to 24 months? An insured account pays a variable rate without exposing the principal to market risk.

Cash above the standard limit: the standard FDIC insurance amount is $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. Larger balances can be spread across banks, or held through a sweep program that uses several program banks, such as Wealthfront (up to $8 million across its program banks, according to Wealthfront).

Bridge cash: money waiting between selling one investment and buying another can sit in an insured account or a brokerage cash option meanwhile.

What Money Market Accounts Are NOT For

Long-term wealth building: the rate is variable and interest is taxable. Money you will not touch for many years usually belongs in investments, which carry risk of loss.

Retirement savings: tax-advantaged accounts such as a Roth IRA or 401(k) are the usual home for long-term retirement money. Interest on a money market account is taxable income each year.

Money you will not need for five or more years: money with a long timeline usually belongs in investments suited to your risk tolerance, not only in cash.

Best Cash Management Options in Brokerage Accounts

Brokerage Cash Options: FDIC-Insured, High Yield, and Liquidity Compared

When you park cash in a brokerage account, the three things to check are insurance, yield and how fast you can use the money. Brokerage cash comes in different forms that are insured differently:

  • Bank sweep programs move your cash to program banks. FDIC insurance applies at each program bank (up to $250,000 per ownership category), subject to the broker's stated limits, and it covers a failure of those banks only. Vanguard's Cash Plus bank sweep works this way.
  • Money market funds are not FDIC insured. SIPC covers up to $500,000, including $250,000 for cash, if your brokerage fails, but it does not protect against a decline in the value of your securities. Yields change daily. Figures read on the funds' own pages on November 28, 2026:
    • Vanguard Federal Money Market Fund (VMFXX): 7-day SEC yield 3.93% as of 11/27/2026; expense ratio 0.11% as of 12/19/2025; $3,000 minimum investment.
    • Fidelity Government Money Market Fund (SPAXX): 7-day yield 3.62% as of 11/27/2026; gross expense ratio 0.42% as of 06/29/2026.
    • Schwab Prime Advantage Money Fund Investor (SWVXX): 7-day yield 3.81% with waivers as of 11/27/2026; net expense ratio 0.340%; no initial minimum. This is a prime fund, which can hold corporate debt, unlike the government funds above, so it takes a different kind of risk.
  • Cash accounts that use program banks (such as Wealthfront's) spread your deposits across partner banks, each providing its own $250,000 of FDIC coverage. Wealthfront shows 3.55% APY as of November 13, 2026 and says that rate can change.

Because money market fund yields move with short-term rates and bank sweep rates are set by the broker, the two can differ by a wide margin. Compare the 7-day yield and the expense ratio of the fund against the sweep rate your broker pays, and decide whether the lack of FDIC insurance on the fund is acceptable for that money.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a money market account safe?

At an FDIC-insured bank or NCUA-insured credit union, yes up to the limit: $250,000 per depositor, per insured institution, per ownership category. Above that, or in a money market fund, the insurance does not apply.

What is the difference between a money market account and a money market fund?

An account is a bank or credit union deposit insured by the FDIC or NCUA. A fund is an investment regulated by the SEC and not insured; its price targets $1.00 but can fall, and its 7-day yield changes.

How much does a money market account pay?

It varies by bank and changes over time. On November 28, 2026, Sallie Mae showed 3.60% APY and Ally 3.20% (as of November 24). The FDIC publishes a national average for money market accounts of 0.64% as of November 16, 2026, so rates vary a lot between banks.

Can I write checks from a money market account?

Often yes. Ally includes checks and a debit card. Check the bank's transaction limits first, because checks and debit card purchases often count toward the limit.

The Bottom Line

If you want cash that is insured, a money market account at an FDIC- or NCUA-insured institution is a sound place for an emergency fund and short-term savings. Check the live APY before opening one. For scale, the gap between 0.64% (the FDIC's national average for money market accounts) and Sallie Mae's 3.60% on $10,000 is about $296 a year in interest before taxes, if the rates stayed the same for a year, which they will not.
