Best Long-Term Investments for the Next 10 Years

The best long-term investments for the next 10 years: low-cost index funds, REITs, TIPS and I bonds, held in a 401(k), IRA or HSA first.

For most people, the best long-term investments for the next 10 years are low-cost index funds (a total US market or S&P 500 fund plus an international fund) rounded out with REITs and inflation-protected Treasuries. Hold them inside a 401(k), IRA or HSA first. None of it is exciting; all of it is cheap, diversified and easy to hold for a decade.

Most investors spend their time chasing the next hot stock, the next crypto surge, the next "can't miss" opportunity. Many of them end up behind a simple index fund.

The best long-term investments are boring. They compound quietly. They don't make headlines. And over 10 years, they build serious wealth for the people disciplined enough to hold them.

Here's where the evidence points.

1. Total Market Index Funds

The single most reliable long-term investment for most people is a low-cost total market index fund. It gives you ownership of the entire US stock market (thousands of companies) for a few hundredths of a percent a year.

The case is about cost, not magic. An index fund doesn't try to beat the market; it is the market, minus a tiny fee. An actively managed fund has to beat the market by more than its higher fees just to keep up, and many don't.

The three-fund portfolio (US total market, international, and bonds) is the foundation most serious long-term investors build on. See our full breakdown: How to Build a Three-Fund Portfolio.

Best options: VTI (Vanguard Morningstar Total Stock Market ETF, 0.03% expense ratio), FSKAX (Fidelity Total Market Index Fund, 0.015%) and SWTSX (Schwab Total Stock Market Index Fund, 0.03%). All three charge under 0.05% a year, per their current fund documents. VTI was formerly called Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF: in 2026 Vanguard added "Morningstar" to the names of its funds that track the former CRSP indexes, after Morningstar rebranded the CRSP indexes as Morningstar Indexes. Vanguard says the name change does not affect how the funds are managed.

What to expect: nobody can promise a 10-year return. For scale, VTI returned 14.70% a year at NAV over the 10 years through September 30, 2026, but 9.44% a year since its 2001 launch, per Vanguard's fact sheet. A strong past decade is not a forecast for the next one.

2. S&P 500 Index Funds

If you want slightly more concentration in large-cap US companies (which have historically driven the most consistent returns), S&P 500 index funds are the classic choice.

In his 2013 letter to Berkshire Hathaway shareholders, Warren Buffett wrote that his advice to the trustee of his wife's inheritance is to put 10% of the cash in short-term government bonds and 90% in a very low-cost S&P 500 index fund. That's not a throwaway comment: it's the conclusion of someone who has spent a lifetime picking investments.

3. Real Estate via REITs

Direct real estate ownership builds wealth but requires capital, management, and concentration risk. REITs (Real Estate Investment Trusts) give you real estate exposure through the stock market: no landlord headaches, no down payment, full liquidity.

To qualify, a REIT must distribute at least 90% of its taxable income to shareholders each year as dividends, which makes REITs a natural income source. Real estate also doesn't always move with the broader stock market, which can help diversification.

For a full breakdown of how to add real estate to your portfolio without buying property, see: How to Invest in REITs for Real Estate Exposure.

Best options: VNQ (Vanguard Real Estate ETF), SCHH (Schwab US REIT ETF). Best held in tax-advantaged accounts, since REIT dividends generally are taxed as ordinary income rather than at the lower rates for qualified dividends.

4. International Index Funds

US stocks have led for much of the past decade. But leadership rotates. International developed markets (Europe, Japan, Australia) and emerging markets (India, Brazil, Southeast Asia) give you a share of businesses a US-only fund never owns.

Many evidence-based portfolios keep a meaningful slice outside the US. Not because international will definitely outperform, but because concentration in any single country adds risk that global diversification removes.

Best options: VXUS (Vanguard Total International Stock ETF), IXUS (iShares Core MSCI Total International Stock ETF).

5. I Bonds and TIPS (Inflation Protection)

For the portion of your portfolio that needs to be safe from inflation (emergency reserves, near-term goals), I bonds and TIPS provide returns tied to the Consumer Price Index.

I bonds are sold through TreasuryDirect and backed by the US government. Bonds issued from November 1, 2026 to April 30, 2027 earn a 5.51% composite rate, which includes a 1.40% fixed rate; the rate resets every six months. The limits: up to $10,000 in electronic I bonds per Social Security number per calendar year, no cashing in for the first 12 months, and if you cash in before 5 years you lose the last 3 months of interest.

TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities) don't have the I bond's $10,000 yearly cap (TreasuryDirect accepts noncompetitive bids of up to $10 million) and come in 5-, 10- and 30-year terms. Most people hold them through a brokerage ETF such as TIP (iShares TIPS Bond ETF) or SCHP.

6. Maxing Tax-Advantaged Accounts First

This isn't an investment category: it's where you hold your investments. But it matters as much as what you invest in.

A 7% annual return inside a Roth IRA grows tax-free, and qualified withdrawals are tax-free too. The same return in a taxable brokerage account is taxed when you sell: long-term capital gains at 0%, 15% or 20% depending on your income, and higher earners can owe an extra 3.8% net investment income tax. Over 10 years, that difference compounds.

The Roth IRA also has income limits. For 2027, the ability to contribute phases out between $158,000 and $173,000 of modified AGI for single filers and heads of household, and between $250,000 and $260,000 for married couples filing jointly. Savers 50 and older can add catch-up contributions on top of these limits.

What should you avoid over the next 10 years?

As important as what to own is what not to own:

  • Individual stocks without edge: picking stocks is a negative-sum game against professionals. Index funds win by not playing.
  • High-fee actively managed funds: assuming a 7% annual return before fees, a 1% expense ratio leaves you with roughly 24% less after 30 years than a 0.03% index fund.
  • Crypto as a core holding: a small speculative position is a choice some investors make with money they can afford to lose. Betting your retirement on it is not.
  • Market timing: no one consistently calls tops and bottoms. Sitting in cash and missing just a handful of the market's best days can do lasting damage to a decade of returns.

The Honest 10-Year Outlook

No one can tell you which asset will perform best over the next 10 years. Anyone who claims otherwise is selling something.

What the evidence does support: diversified ownership of productive assets (businesses via index funds, real estate via REITs, inflation protection via TIPS), held in tax-advantaged accounts, with low fees and rebalanced annually, gives most people better odds than trying to outguess the market.

That's not exciting. It's just true.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the single best investment to hold for 10 years?

For most people, a low-cost total US market or S&P 500 index fund such as VTI, FSKAX, VOO or FXAIX, each charging 0.03% a year or less. Add an international fund and some bonds as your situation calls for them.

How much can I put in a 401(k), IRA or HSA in 2027?

For 2027 the IRS limits are $25,500 for a 401(k), $7,500 for an IRA, and $4,500 for an HSA with self-only coverage or $9,000 with family coverage. Savers 50 and older can contribute more through catch-up contributions.

Are I bonds worth buying right now?

I bonds issued from November 1, 2026 to April 30, 2027 earn 5.51%, including a 1.40% fixed rate. They suit money you can leave alone for at least a year, since you can't cash them in for 12 months and lose 3 months of interest if you cash in before 5 years.

Should I hold REITs in a taxable account or an IRA?

An IRA or other tax-advantaged account is usually the better home, because REIT dividends generally are taxed as ordinary income rather than at the lower qualified-dividend rates.
