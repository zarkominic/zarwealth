Why robo-advisor portfolios matter

A robo-advisor builds and rebalances a diversified portfolio for you automatically, usually for a fraction of what a human advisor charges. For most people, this is enough: low-cost funds, automatic rebalancing, tax-loss harvesting on taxable accounts, and goal-based planning. No fund-picking, fewer behavioral mistakes, no expensive advisor.

The catch? Not all robo-advisors are built the same. Advisory fees range from $0 (Schwab Intelligent Portfolios, and Fidelity Go under $25,000) to 0.65% (Betterment Premium, which adds access to financial experts). Some let you customize, some use a fixed portfolio. This guide compares six well-known names in the US so you can pick the right one for your situation.

If you are completely new to the concept, our robo-advisor for retirement planning guide walks through the basics first.

How we compared the portfolios

I evaluated each platform on five criteria that actually matter for long-term returns: the advisory fee (the percentage charged annually on assets), the minimum balance, tax-loss harvesting (which can lower your tax bill on taxable accounts), portfolio customization, and goal planning quality (does the platform tell you how much to save to reach a goal?).

I did not weight "AI features" or fancy dashboards: those are marketing, not math. What moves the needle over 30 years is fees, taxes, and rebalancing discipline.

The 6 best robo-advisor portfolios (reviewed)

Each review follows the same format: what the portfolio actually does, who it's for, what to skip, and the bottom line. If you are short on time, picks #1 and #2 cover most typical investors.

1. Betterment: Best Overall Portfolio

Betterment pioneered the modern robo-advisor model. Its core portfolio is a globally diversified mix of stock and bond ETFs, weighted by your risk tolerance and goal timeline.

What the portfolio actually does: automatic rebalancing, tax-loss harvesting on taxable accounts, tax-coordinated asset location across taxable and retirement accounts (bonds in IRAs, stocks in taxable), and goal-based projections (retirement, house, emergency fund).

Who it's for: anyone who wants the most mature, well-rounded robo-advisor without thinking about features. Especially good if you have multiple goals and want them tracked separately. What to skip: the Premium tier (0.65% on the first $1 million, $100,000 minimum, with access to financial experts). Unless you have a genuinely complex situation, the 0.25% Digital tier is enough. Note that below $24,000 Betterment charges a flat $5/month unless you set up recurring deposits of $200 or more a month. The bottom line: if you cannot decide, pick Betterment. It is the safe default.

2. Wealthfront: Best for Direct Indexing

Wealthfront competes head-to-head with Betterment: same 0.25% advisory fee, a similar diversified ETF portfolio, and a $500 minimum. Its edge is in taxable accounts: in the automated portfolio, accounts over $100,000 can add direct indexing, which replaces the US stock ETF with the individual stocks it holds so more losses can be harvested. Wealthfront also sells standalone direct-indexing portfolios that track the S&P 500 (0.09% a year) or the Nasdaq-100 (0.12%), with a $5,000 minimum.

What the portfolio actually does: diversified ETF allocation across US and international stocks and bonds, automatic rebalancing, automatic tax-loss harvesting, and built-in goal planning tools. Uninvested money can sit in the Wealthfront Cash Account, which paid a base 3.55% APY as of November 13, 2026 (variable), with FDIC insurance eligibility of up to $8 million through program banks.

Who it's for: investors with large taxable accounts who want the maximum after-tax return. Also good for quant-curious people: Wealthfront publishes white papers on its approach. The bottom line: if your taxable balance is large, Wealthfront's direct indexing alone is a strong reason to choose it over Betterment.

3. Schwab Intelligent Portfolios: Best for Fee-Free Management

Schwab's robo charges no advisory fee and no commissions, with a $5,000 minimum. The catch: part of every portfolio is held in cash at Schwab Bank, and Schwab says it does not charge an advisory fee in part because of the revenue the bank earns on that cash. In the example allocations Schwab shows on its Intelligent Portfolios page, cash ranges from 6.9% to 15%. Tax-loss harvesting is available once you have $50,000 or more invested.

What the portfolio actually does: an ETF allocation across stocks, bonds, REITs, commodities such as gold, and cash, with automatic rebalancing.

Who it's for: Schwab customers who already have brokerage accounts there and want a hands-off option without paying an advisory fee. What to skip: if you would rather be fully invested, the cash allocation is a real cost even if it never shows up as a fee. The bottom line: the "free" pricing model is genuinely free of advisory fees, but only worth it if you understand the cash trade-off.

4. Vanguard Digital Advisor: Best for Bogleheads

Vanguard's robo charges a gross advisory fee of 0.20% for its all-index option (0.25% for the active/index option), reduced by a credit for the revenue Vanguard earns on its own funds in your account. Vanguard puts the result at about $15–$16 a year per $10,000 in an all-index portfolio. You need $100 to enroll.

What the portfolio actually does: a portfolio of low-cost Vanguard ETFs across US and international stock and bond markets, with all-index, active/index and ESG options. It rebalances if your portfolio drifts more than 5% from the recommended allocation, and tax-loss harvesting is included at no extra cost. If you want a human, Vanguard Personal Advisor adds ongoing planning and access to an advisor for those investing $50,000 or more.

Who it's for: Bogleheads who want a low-cost, simple robo built on Vanguard funds. Also a natural fit for IRA money already at Vanguard. The bottom line: one of the cheapest ways to get a managed portfolio. If "low fees, leave me alone" is your priority, this is it.

5. Fidelity Go: Best for Beginners

Fidelity Go charges no advisory fee under $25,000, then 0.35% a year at $25,000 and above. There is no minimum to open an account, and you need $10 to start investing. Portfolios are built from mutual funds, including Fidelity Flex funds.

What the portfolio actually does: a mix of stock and bond funds matched to your goals, timeline and risk comfort, rebalanced automatically. Once you reach $25,000, taxable accounts get tax-loss harvesting and you unlock unlimited one-on-one coaching sessions. It is built into the Fidelity app, so the experience is seamless if you already bank or invest there.

Who it's for: someone starting their first investment account with under $25,000 who wants a true zero-advisory-fee option. What to weigh: at $25,000 the 0.35% fee is higher than Betterment's or Wealthfront's 0.25%, so compare the coaching and harvesting you get against the extra cost. The bottom line: the best on-ramp for beginners.

6. M1 Finance: Best for Custom Portfolios

M1 is technically not a "pure" robo: you choose what you own (the "Pie" model), then M1 automates investing and rebalancing toward your targets. M1 does not charge commissions or management fees on self-directed brokerage accounts, but it does charge a $3/month platform fee, waived for clients with more than $10,000 on the platform.

What the portfolio actually does: you build a Pie of stocks and ETFs with target percentages, using fractional shares. New money goes toward whatever is underweight to keep your target allocation. M1 does not offer a tax-loss harvesting option; instead it tries to realize losses before gains when it sells. M1 Advisor, a free advisory service built into M1, can help you decide what to hold, but M1 does not pick a portfolio for you.

Who it's for: investors who want to specify their own portfolio (say, 70% total US market / 20% international / 10% individual stocks) without manual rebalancing. What to skip: if you do not want to make the allocation decisions yourself, a traditional robo will pick for you. The bottom line: the best DIY-with-training-wheels option. Not for purely hands-off investors.

How to choose the right robo-advisor for your situation

The "best" robo depends on three questions: how big is your taxable balance?, how much do you want to customize?, and where do you already bank?

If you have a large taxable balance and want maximum after-tax returns, look at Wealthfront for direct indexing. If you are starting fresh and want the most mature, well-designed experience, pick Betterment. If you want low-cost Vanguard funds and a small fee, pick Vanguard Digital Advisor. If you have under $25,000 and want no advisory fee, start with Fidelity Go. If you want to build a custom portfolio with automated rebalancing, pick M1.

None of these are bad choices for long-term wealth building. The worst decision is staying in cash because you cannot pick.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the cheapest robo-advisor?

Schwab Intelligent Portfolios charges no advisory fee (with a $5,000 minimum and a cash allocation), and Fidelity Go charges none under $25,000. Among fully invested portfolios with a fee, Vanguard Digital Advisor is the cheapest here, at about $15–$16 a year per $10,000 in its all-index option.

Are robo-advisors safe?

Your assets are held in brokerage accounts at SIPC-member firms. SIPC protects up to $500,000 per customer, including a $250,000 limit for cash, if the brokerage fails. It does not protect against market losses. The "robo" part only affects how the portfolio is managed.

Should I use a robo-advisor or invest myself in index funds?

If you would actually rebalance yearly, harvest tax losses, and resist behavioral mistakes during a 30% drawdown, DIY index funds will save you the fee. If you would not (most people), paying a robo to do it for you can easily be worth it in avoided mistakes.

Can robo-advisors handle Roth IRA accounts?

Many robo-advisors do (Schwab and M1, for example, list IRAs alongside taxable accounts), but check each provider's list of account types, especially if you need a SEP IRA. Tax-loss harvesting does not apply inside IRAs (there are no capital gains taxes to offset), so harvesting features matter only for taxable money.

How long does it take to open a robo-advisor account?

The application itself is short and online: name, address, Social Security number, and a linked bank account. Betterment has no minimum balance and Fidelity Go has no minimum to open; Wealthfront needs $500 and Schwab $5,000 before your money is invested.

Do robo-advisors beat the S&P 500?

Sometimes yes, often no. The benchmark for a typical balanced robo portfolio (60% stocks/40% bonds) is not the S&P 500: it's a blended index that includes international stocks and bonds. Comparing to the S&P 500 alone is unfair both ways. What robos do well: keep you from selling at the bottom of a crash.

What changed

Updated November 24, 2026, from the September 20, 2026 version:

  • Vanguard Digital Advisor now lists tax-loss harvesting at no extra cost and rebalances on a 5% drift; our earlier "no tax-loss harvesting, annual rebalancing only" was wrong. Its fee is now stated as Vanguard does: 0.20% gross for the index option, about $15–$16 per $10,000 net.
  • Fidelity Go offers tax-loss harvesting and one-on-one coaching once a taxable account reaches $25,000; we previously said it had no harvesting on any tier.
  • Schwab Intelligent Portfolios needs $50,000 invested for tax-loss harvesting (we said $5,000).
  • M1: we removed the M1 Plus subscription and "Expert Pies," which we could not find on M1's current homepage and pricing FAQ. M1 now lists a $3/month platform fee, waived above $10,000, and a free M1 Advisor service.
  • Wealthfront's Cash Account base rate is 3.55% APY (as of November 13, 2026), down from the roughly 4.15% we listed in September.
  • Removed figures we could not confirm on the providers' own pages (assets under management, ETF counts, Smart Beta and Betterment direct indexing thresholds, Schwab's old 6–30% cash range and the Schwab Intelligent Portfolios Premium price and status, expected tax-harvesting gains), and added a fee table.
