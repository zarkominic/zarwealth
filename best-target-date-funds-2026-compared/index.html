Target date funds are a common default investment in 401(k) plans, and for good reason. Pick a fund with a year close to your retirement, set automatic contributions, and you're done. The fund handles asset allocation, rebalancing, and gradual risk reduction automatically.

But not all target date funds are equal. Expense ratios on this list range from 0.08% to 0.70% depending on the provider and share class. Glide paths (how aggressive the fund is at each age) differ meaningfully. And the "best" target date fund for you depends on which year you'll retire, and which share classes your brokerage account or 401(k) plan actually offers.

This guide compares seven target date fund series using the 2055 vintage of each, with every expense ratio taken from the fund's current summary prospectus on file with the SEC.

What Makes a Good Target Date Fund in 2027?

Four factors separate excellent target date funds from mediocre ones:

1. Expense ratio. Over 30 years, a fee that is 0.50 percentage points higher leaves you with roughly 14% less money at the end (0.995 raised to the 30th power is about 0.86). The cheapest index series on this list charge 0.08%–0.12%. Anything above 0.50% needs a very good reason: there are too many cheap alternatives.

2. Glide path methodology. "Glide path" = how the stock/bond mix shifts as you age. Some funds keep shifting for years after the target date ("through retirement"): Vanguard's reaches the mix of its Income fund within seven years after the target year, Schwab's keeps adjusting for 20 years, and Fidelity Freedom Index's for roughly 15 to 20 years. Others concentrate the de-risking before retirement. Neither is automatically right, but you should know which one you own.

3. Underlying fund quality. Target date funds are funds-of-funds. The index series (Vanguard, Schwab, Fidelity Freedom Index, BlackRock LifePath Index, State Street) hold low-cost index funds underneath. Actively managed series (Fidelity Freedom, American Funds) hold active funds, which shows up in the fee.

4. Share class and availability. Several of these series come in multiple share classes with different fees, and some classes can only be bought through a financial intermediary or come with conditions. If you're saving in an IRA or taxable account, your options narrow.

The 7 Best Target Date Funds for 2027, Ranked

The best target date funds for 2027 are the low-cost index series: Vanguard Target Retirement, Schwab Target Index, and Fidelity Freedom Index. They charge 0.08%–0.12% in the share classes compared here and automatically glide from stocks to bonds as you near retirement. Below we rank the seven best and show exactly how they differ.

1. Vanguard Target Retirement Series (VTTHX, VTIVX, VFFVX, VLXVX)

Expense ratio: 0.08% (Investor shares). Glide path: through retirement. Within seven years after the target year, each fund's mix becomes similar to Vanguard Target Retirement Income. Why it wins: the prospectus lists a 0.00% management fee; the entire 0.08% is the cost of the underlying Vanguard index funds. The minimum to open an Investor Shares account is generally $1,000.

Vanguard filed summary prospectuses for vintages every 5 years from 2020 to 2070, plus the Income fund. For a 35-year-old planning to retire around 2055, the Vanguard Target Retirement 2055 Fund (VFFVX) is the natural pick.

2. Schwab Target Index Funds (SWYFX, SWYHX, SWYJX, SWYOX)

Expense ratio: 0.08% net. The prospectus shows 0.13% in total expenses before a 0.05% reduction; Schwab's adviser has agreed to cap the fund at 0.08% for as long as it serves as adviser. Glide path: through retirement. The fund keeps reducing stocks for 20 years beyond its target date, then holds about 28% stocks, 68% bonds, and 4% cash. Why it competes: the same net expense ratio as Vanguard. The main reason to pick Schwab over Vanguard is if your brokerage account is already at Schwab.

3. Fidelity Freedom Index Funds (FIHFX, FIOFX, FDEWX, FFIJX)

Expense ratio: 0.12% for the Investor class; the Institutional Premium class (FFLDX for 2055) is 0.08%. Glide path: through retirement. Each fund adjusts until it resembles the Fidelity Freedom Index Retirement Fund, roughly 15 to 20 years after its target year. The 2055 fund's prospectus also says a revised asset allocation strategy is expected to take effect by the end of the first quarter of 2027, and Fidelity has already begun the transition. Why it competes: this is the index version of Fidelity's series. Don't confuse it with the actively managed Fidelity Freedom Funds: Fidelity Freedom 2035 (FFTHX) charges 0.63% and Fidelity Freedom 2040 (FFFFX) charges 0.66%. If you have a Fidelity 401(k) or IRA, check that the fund you're buying has "Index" in its name.

4. BlackRock LifePath Index Funds (LIJKX, LIHKX, LIVKX)

Expense ratio: 0.09% for the 2055 fund's Class K shares (LIVKX), after a fee waiver that runs through June 30, 2027; 0.13% before it. Glide path: the prospectus describes the allocation becoming more conservative as retirement approaches. Why it competes: a low-cost index series with a fee in Vanguard's range. Note that Class K shares are sold through financial intermediaries, so whether you can buy them depends on your plan or broker.

5. State Street Target Retirement Funds (SSDQX, SSDOX)

Expense ratio: 0.09% for the 2055 fund's Class K shares (SSDQX) after a waiver that runs through April 30, 2027 (0.16% before it); Class I shares (SSDOX) cost 0.29% after the same waiver (0.36% without it). Glide path: the prospectus says the adviser adjusts the mix in the years immediately before and after the retirement date. Why it competes: fees close to Vanguard's in the K class. Check which share class your plan offers: the I class costs more than three times as much.

6. T. Rowe Price Retirement Blend Funds (TRBOX, TBLMX)

Expense ratio: 0.43% for the 2055 fund's Investor Class (TRBOX) and 0.25% for its I Class (TBLMX); certain accounts are also charged a $20 annual fee. The management fee is set to decline over time on a contractual schedule. Glide path: through retirement. 55% stocks at the target date, with stock exposure continuing to decline until about 30 years after it. Why it competes: T. Rowe's "Blend" series mixes active and passive management. The fee is higher than the pure index series, so you are paying for T. Rowe's active management. Not for everyone.

7. American Funds Target Date Retirement Series (RFKTX, AAMTX)

Expense ratio: 0.38% for the 2055 fund's R-6 shares (RFKTX); 0.70% for Class A shares (AAMTX), which also carry a front-end sales charge of up to 5.75%. Glide path: through retirement. The fund keeps providing stock and bond exposure after the target date. Why it competes: the only fully actively managed series we'd consider including. Even in the cheapest R-6 class, it costs 0.30 percentage points more than Vanguard (about $300 a year on $100,000), so the active management has to earn that back.

Vanguard vs Fidelity vs Schwab Target Date Funds: Side by Side

Schwab vs Vanguard target date funds: Schwab Target Index and Vanguard Target Retirement both charge 0.08% net. For most investors the choice comes down to which brokerage you already use.

Here's a hypothetical: $100,000 invested in each provider's 2055 fund for 30 years, assuming a 7% annual return before fees (an assumption, not a forecast) and the current expense ratio deducted every year. With no fee at all, it would grow to about $761,000.

Vanguard VFFVX (0.08%): $80 in the first year on $100,000. Ending balance: about $743,000.

Schwab SWYJX (0.08%): Identical to Vanguard: about $743,000.

BlackRock LIVKX (0.09%, Class K): About $741,000.

Fidelity FDEWX (0.12%, Investor): $120 in the first year on $100,000. Ending balance: about $734,000.

T. Rowe Price TRBOX (0.43%, Investor): About $669,000.

The differences look small but compound: in this example, Vanguard or Schwab ends about $9,000 ahead of Fidelity's Investor class and about $74,000 ahead of the T. Rowe Price Investor class.

How to Choose the Right Target Date Fund for Your Age

The rule of thumb: pick the fund with a year matching your expected retirement. Fidelity's prospectus, for example, assumes retirement at or around age 65.

  • If you're 25 (born ~2002): target 2065 fund: VLXVX (Vanguard), SWYOX (Schwab), FFIJX (Fidelity Freedom Index).
  • If you're 35 (born ~1992): target 2055 fund: VFFVX, SWYJX, FDEWX.
  • If you're 45 (born ~1982): target 2045 fund: VTIVX, SWYHX, FIOFX.
  • If you're 55 (born ~1972): target 2035 fund: VTTHX, SWYFX, FIHFX.
  • If you're 65 or already retired: an income-stage fund: VTINX (Vanguard Target Retirement Income) or FIKFX (Fidelity Freedom Index Retirement Fund, formerly Freedom Index Income).

If you're aggressive and want more equity exposure than your age suggests, pick the fund 5-10 years later than your retirement (e.g., a 35-year-old picks Target 2065 instead of 2055). If you're conservative, pick 5-10 years earlier.

Target Date Fund vs Three-Fund Portfolio: Which Is Better?

The honest answer: for most investors, a target date fund is better than a three-fund portfolio. Reasons:

  1. You won't tinker. Target date funds rebalance automatically. A common way three-fund portfolios go wrong is investors not rebalancing (or rebalancing at the wrong time).
  2. The fee difference is small. Vanguard Target Retirement charges no management fee of its own: its prospectus lists 0.00% for management and puts the whole 0.08% on the underlying index funds. Building the same mix yourself saves little, if anything.
  3. Behavior matters more than allocation. A target date fund saves you from yourself in a market crash. Investors who DIY a three-fund portfolio often panic-sell in downturns, locking in losses the target date fund would have ridden out.

The case for a three-fund portfolio is mostly for people who genuinely enjoy the optimization process or have very specific tax-loss harvesting needs. For everyone else, the target date fund wins on behavioral grounds. See our guide on how to build a three-fund portfolio if you want to compare the two approaches in detail.

Best Target Date Funds for 2027 FAQ

What is the best target date fund for 2027?

For most investors it's the Vanguard Target Retirement fund matching your retirement year, at 0.08% for Investor shares. It holds Vanguard's index funds underneath, keeps adjusting for up to seven years after the target year, and the minimum to open an account is generally $1,000. Schwab Target Index Funds charge the same 0.08% net and are the right choice if your accounts are already at Schwab.

Vanguard vs Fidelity target date funds: which is better in 2027?

Vanguard wins on fees: 0.08% vs 0.12% for Fidelity Freedom Index Investor shares, and far less than the actively managed Fidelity Freedom Funds (0.63% for Freedom 2035, 0.66% for Freedom 2040). Fidelity's Institutional Premium class of Freedom Index also costs 0.08%; which class you can buy depends on your account or plan. If you have a Fidelity 401(k), choose the Freedom Index funds over the active Freedom funds.

How much of my portfolio should be in a target date fund?

If you choose a target date fund, it should typically be 100% of your retirement contributions. Target date funds are designed as complete portfolios: splitting your savings between a target date fund and individual ETFs typically just complicates rebalancing without improving returns. The exception: holding individual sector or value ETFs in a taxable account for tax-loss harvesting, while keeping your 401(k) entirely in a target date fund.

Are target date funds good for early retirement (FIRE)?

Mixed. Target date funds are built around a traditional retirement age (Fidelity's prospectus assumes about 65): too conservative for someone targeting 45-50. FIRE-seekers often pick a target date fund 10-15 years later than their actual retirement to keep equity exposure higher. Alternatively, they self-manage a three-fund or four-fund portfolio with a higher stock allocation. Our guide on the FIRE movement covers this tradeoff in depth.

What is a glide path in a target date fund?

A glide path is the formula that determines how a target date fund's stock-to-bond ratio shifts as you age. Real examples from current prospectuses: Schwab's 2055 fund held about 92.6% stocks as of February 1, 2026 and will end at about 28% stocks 20 years after 2055; T. Rowe Price's Retirement Blend funds hold 55% stocks at the target date and keep reducing for about 30 years. "Through retirement" paths keep adjusting after the target year; "to retirement" paths do most of their de-risking before it.

Can I buy a target date fund in a Roth IRA?

Yes. Vanguard, Schwab, Fidelity, and most brokerages let you buy target date funds in IRAs (Roth or Traditional). For Vanguard Target Retirement Investor Shares, the minimum to open an account is generally $1,000. Minimums at other providers vary by fund and share class, so check the fund's page before you buy.

What is the difference between Vanguard Target Retirement and Target Retirement Income?

Vanguard Target Retirement (e.g., 2055, 2045) is for people still saving for a future retirement. Vanguard Target Retirement Income (VTINX) is designed for investors already in retirement and is the most conservative of the series: as of September 30, 2025, it held about 31% stocks (18.5% U.S. and 12.9% foreign) with the rest in bonds and inflation-indexed securities. Each dated fund's mix becomes similar to the Income fund within seven years after its target year.

The Bottom Line on Target Date Funds in 2027

For most investors, the choice is simple: Vanguard Target Retirement Series, matched to your retirement year. 0.08% fee, through-retirement glide path, low-cost underlying index funds. If your brokerage is already at Schwab, use Schwab Target Index Funds: same 0.08% net fee. If you have a Fidelity 401(k), use Fidelity Freedom Index Funds, but verify the fund name includes "Index", not the 0.63%–0.66% actively managed Freedom Funds checked above.

Avoid actively managed target date funds with fees above 0.50% unless you have a clear reason. Over a 30-year horizon, the fee drag is hard for active management to overcome.
